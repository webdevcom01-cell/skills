# Week 0 baseline — measuring so the Week 12 number survives scrutiny

## Why this is the hardest week to sell and the most important to run

Clients resist Week 0 because it feels like a week of no progress. The counter-argument is short: the entire commercial case for training a team rather than buying a build rests on a single claim — that hours went down. Without a before number, that claim is an opinion, and the client's CFO will treat it as one.

There is also a quieter benefit. The measurement itself frequently changes what gets automated first, because sponsors are routinely wrong about where their team's hours actually go. Discovering that in Week 0 costs nothing; discovering it in Week 9 wastes two months.

## What to measure

For each participant, their **five most repeated tasks**, and for each task:

| Field | Notes |
|---|---|
| Task name | In the team's own words, not reworded into consultant language |
| Tool used today | The specific named system |
| Times per week | How often it happens |
| Minutes per occurrence | Their estimate is acceptable — see method note below |
| Hours per week | Calculated, not entered |
| Who else does this | Reveals duplicated effort across the team |
| Annoyance (1–5) | Predicts adoption better than hours do |

That last column matters more than it looks. People automate what irritates them, not what is objectively expensive. A 6-hour task rated 1 for annoyance often stays manual all year, while a 40-minute task rated 5 gets automated in Week 5 and stays used.

## Method — the rule that keeps the numbers honest

**Whatever method is used in Week 0 must be used identically in Week 12.** Self-estimation is fine. Time-tracking is fine. Mixing them is not — a self-estimated before and a tracked after produces a fake improvement, and it is the most common way enablement ROI figures get quietly inflated.

Self-estimation is the recommended default: it is fast, it is what people will actually do, and its bias is roughly constant across both measurements, which is what matters for a comparison.

Practical guidance for the session:

- Collect over **one representative working week**, not one day. Days are too noisy, months lose too many people to attrition.
- Have people log at the end of each day rather than reconstructing the week on Friday. Friday reconstruction systematically underestimates small repeated tasks — exactly the ones this programme targets.
- Cap the exercise at five tasks per person. Ten tasks produces worse data, not more, because attention degrades.
- Avoid framing it as a productivity audit. If people suspect the numbers will be used to judge them, the numbers become useless. Say plainly what it is for: choosing which tasks to attack first, and proving at the end whether it worked.

## The rollup

Total hours per task across all participants. The sheet ships with filter arrows on the header row; sort by total hours descending before reviewing it. This ranking drives Week 5 — the first skill each participant writes targets a task near the top of their own list, because a first skill that saves twenty minutes a week does not survive contact with a busy month.

Cross-check the rollup against the annoyance scores. When a task is high-hours and low-annoyance, expect resistance; when it is mid-hours and high-annoyance, expect enthusiasm. Sequence accordingly — early wins buy tolerance for the harder weeks.

**Check the UNMATCHED row before anything else.** It shows hours logged against a task name that is not on the rollup, and it turns red when it is above zero. That is almost always a typo or a task somebody discovered mid-week. Add the task as a row, or correct the spelling in the log — but do not present a rollup with unmatched hours sitting in it, because the per-task numbers will not add up to the total and the sponsor will notice.

## Sponsor sign-off

The sponsor reviews the rollup before Week 1 and confirms the numbers look plausible. This takes ten minutes and does two things: it prevents a Week 12 dispute about the baseline being wrong, and it usually surprises the sponsor, which is when they start paying attention.

Record the sign-off date in the workbook. An unsigned baseline is a baseline the client can disown later.

## What Week 12 does with this

Re-run the identical measurement. The comparison sheet computes, per task, the delta in hours per week and shades red any row where hours went **up** — which happens, and is a genuine finding rather than an embarrassment. A skill that added work is a skill to kill, and finding it is the programme working correctly.

Report three numbers, not one: hours saved per week, the number of tasks that improved out of those attempted, and the number of artefacts killed. A single headline number invites disbelief; three numbers that include a negative read as an honest account.

## Gaps and how to mark them

If a participant cannot estimate a task, write `NOT MEASURED` in the **Notes** column and leave the number columns blank — a blank row is already excluded from the totals, so nothing else is needed. Do not put `NOT MEASURED` in the task-name column: that changes the name, breaks the match, and moves any hours on that row into UNMATCHED.

One clearly marked gap is a minor caveat. One invented number, discovered later, retroactively discredits every other number in the document.
