# Engagement terms, disclaimers and the employee notice

Everything in this file exists because three independent reviewers of a finished
programme — a consultant, a role-played client and an adversarial reviewer — landed
on the same verdict from different directions: the thinking was sound and the
commercial terms were absent. The client's line was the useful one. *"The thinking is
an 8. The commercial terms are a 2, because there are none."*

A programme document without terms is a curriculum, not a proposal. There is nothing
to sign, and worse, the plan starts reading as the contract — which is dangerous,
because the plan is full of conditions on the client and none on the consultant.

---

## 1. The engagement terms annex

Produce this as a **fifth deliverable**, `<client>-engagement-terms.md`, alongside the
programme. It does not replace a contract; it makes the shape of the deal explicit so
the contract can be written, and it stops the programme document being read as one.

Cover every heading below. Where a value is unknown, write `[TO AGREE]` rather than
omitting the heading — a missing heading looks like an oversight, a marked blank looks
like an open item.

**What the fee covers.** Number of facilitated sessions, session length, delivery mode
(on site or remote — this changes the price substantially and clients assume the
cheaper one), the named deliverables, and how many revisions of each.

**What it does not cover.** Legal or compliance advice, data protection assessments,
IT security work, tool licences and subscriptions, building or operating anything
outside the sessions, and any work arising from the programme's findings. Be
specific; a vague exclusion protects nobody.

**Fees and payment.** Amount, schedule, and whether anything is held back. Consider
offering a **stop point after Week 6** with nothing further owed — clients who will
not commit to fourteen weeks up front will commit to six, and a programme that has to
re-earn its second half is a stronger promise than one that does not.

**Fees are not contingent on measured outcomes.** State this explicitly. The
programme's own logic makes hours-saved the headline number, and a client can
reasonably read that as a guarantee unless it is denied in writing. If a
success-linked element is being offered, tie it to something the consultant controls —
sessions delivered, participants able to run a tested skill unaided, ownership
register complete — never to a self-reported hours figure.

**Client dependencies, and what happens when they are not met.** Sponsor attendance,
participant availability, tool access, the baseline being completed, decisions on the
open items. Name the consequence of each: which week slips, and who bears the cost.

**Rescheduling and no-shows.** Two moves per side is a reasonable default. State which
side bears the cost in each case. Without this, a fourteen-week programme becomes a
twenty-two-week one at the consultant's expense.

**Named consultant and substitution.** Clients buy a person. If someone else may
deliver sessions, say so now.

**Tool and licence costs.** The programme depends on the client holding an AI
subscription capable of the connections in Week 4 and the scheduling in Week 8, per
seat, recurring. Name it as a client cost before Week 0 and estimate it. There is an
awkwardness worth being honest about: the pitch is that training is paid for once
while software is paid for forever, and the training itself sits on a perpetual
per-seat subscription.

**Intellectual property, both directions.** Who owns the skills, prompts, acceptance
checks and context documents the team writes — the sensible answer is the client — and
a commitment that the client's own material does not turn up in another client's
programme.

**Confidentiality and non-solicitation.** The consultant spends fourteen weeks
learning which of the client's people are the strongest. Clients notice this. Offering
the term before being asked is worth more than conceding it later.

**Liability cap.** Conventionally the fee. Say so.

Put one line at the top of the programme document itself: *"This plan is a working
document, not a contract. The engagement terms annex and the signed agreement
govern."*

## 2. The Week 0 employee notice

Week 0 collects, per named employee, how long each of their tasks takes and how often
they do it, and hands the totals to the person who signs their pay. That is employee
data by any reading, and the programme creates it.

The skill does not give legal advice and neither should the deliverable. What it does
is refuse to collect the data until the client has told its own people what is
happening — which is both decent and the thing most likely to keep the numbers honest,
since a workforce that suspects a performance audit produces useless data.

**Before any measurement, the client — not the consultant — issues a short written
notice to participants covering:**

- What is being collected and for how long
- Why: choosing which tasks to work on first, and checking at the end whether it worked
- Who sees the totals, and in what form
- How long the workbook is kept and when it is deleted
- That it will not be used in individual performance assessment
- Who to ask about it

**Then confirm, before running the workbook:** the notice went out, and where employee
representatives or a works council exist, they were consulted. In several European
jurisdictions a measurement instrument of this kind engages consultation obligations,
and consent given by an employee to an employer is treated cautiously because of the
power imbalance. **Whether and how that applies to a specific client is a question for
the client's own adviser — do not assert it in a deliverable, and do not proceed on the
assumption that it does not apply.**

Two design choices reduce the exposure considerably and cost nothing:

- **Use initials or role labels rather than full names** in the workbook. The Week 12
  comparison works identically; the client holds the mapping.
- **Say plainly in the workbook what the data will not be used for** — and get the
  purpose limitation into the engagement terms, not just into a verbal reassurance the
  consultant scripts for the sponsor. A promise the consultant cannot enforce, made to
  employees on the client's behalf, is worse than no promise.

## 3. Claims that must never appear in a deliverable

These are absolute. If a sentence in a draft does one of these, rewrite it.

**Never state or imply that any configuration is compliant, lawful, defensible,
approved, or sufficient for a regulator.** Not "this is the only version that can be
defended before the supervisory authority", not "this satisfies the requirement", not
"this keeps you compliant". Rewrite as a risk statement: *"this configuration reduces
exposure; whether it is sufficient is a legal determination for your adviser."*
Sufficiency is exactly what the client will quote back after an inspection.

**Never name a statute, regulator or legal rule the consultant cannot cite.** Write
`[REGULATOR / RULE — CONFIRM WITH THE CLIENT'S ADVISER]` instead. A named law that
turns out to be the wrong one destroys the credibility of everything around it, and in
several jurisdictions paid legal classification is itself restricted activity.

**Never cite industry research without naming the study, its sample and the criticism
of it.** "Most AI projects fail" and "research shows" are the same failure the
programme's own Week 2 rule forbids the client from committing. Cite it properly or
say nothing about it.

**Never promise an outcome the consultant does not control.** "A programme that leaves
your team able to build and maintain their AI tools" is a guarantee. "A programme
designed to transfer that capability, with the conditions it depends on stated below"
is a commitment. The difference is the client's exhibit A.

**Never invent a fact about the client.** Not their process, not their volumes, not
their systems, not what their people find annoying. If the brief did not supply it,
mark it as an assumption to confirm — inside the document, where the client sees it,
not only in the covering email.

## 4. The mandatory legal notice

Every deliverable that names a law, a regulator, or a data category carries this
block, in the client's language, unchanged:

> *These are working rules, not a legal opinion. They have not been prepared or
> reviewed by a lawyer. Before adopting them, have your legal adviser or data
> protection officer confirm what applies to your organisation and to the
> jurisdictions you operate in.*

Two rules about it. It goes at the **top** of the governance card, not the last page —
a disclaimer that appears after the client has read and acted on the content is
decoration. And a governance card containing any unfilled `[TO CONFIRM]` field is
watermarked **DRAFT — DO NOT PRINT** with the print instruction suppressed, because
Step 6's covering email is read once while the card goes on a wall for a year.

## 5. What the programme does not produce

Include this in the programme document, named plainly. A client who has paid for
fourteen weeks of AI work will otherwise conclude that "the AI data question is
handled" — the exact false assurance the governance card is meant to prevent,
manufactured by the programme.

The one-page card is an operating rule set. It is not a data protection impact
assessment, a record of processing, an internal policy, a vendor register, a breach
log or a retention schedule. Where those are needed they sit with the client's adviser
or data protection officer, outside this engagement.

Naming them protects the consultant and is a credible follow-on conversation. Both are
better than the client discovering the gap during an incident.

## 6. The consultant's own data handling

The intake asks for the client's tool stack, their repeated tasks, their staff and
their sensitivity constraints, and the deliverable then hands the client a rule saying
personal data is red-tier and never goes into an AI tool. A client's counsel will spot
that asymmetry during due diligence, and it ends engagements.

Before running the skill on real client data:

- A confidentiality agreement, and a data processing agreement where the consultant
  handles employee data on the client's behalf
- Initials or role labels rather than names in AI sessions and in script arguments
- A stated location for intake material and workbooks, who else can access them, and a
  deletion date — thirty days after Week 13 is a defensible default
- A business-tier AI plan with no training on inputs. This is precisely the condition
  the programme imposes on the client at Week 4, and the client is entitled to ask
  whether the consultant meets it.
