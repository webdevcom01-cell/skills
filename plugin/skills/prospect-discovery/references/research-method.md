# Research method — what to search, in what order, and what each source can prove

## Search order

Each layer answers something the next cannot, and doing them out of order wastes effort — you cannot judge whether a news item matters until you know what the company does.

**1. The company's own site, including subpages.** The home page is marketing; the value is in About, Services or Solutions, Products, Contact, Careers and any Media or News section. Careers pages are underrated — a job advert names tools, team structures and reporting lines that appear nowhere else on the site.

**2. The registry.** Legal name, registration number, incorporation date, status, registered address, officers. This is where a trading name becomes an entity.

**3. Trade, industry or sector data.** For traders and manufacturers, shipment and customs aggregators. For services, professional directories and industry bodies. Indicative, never definitive.

**4. News and public statements.** Press coverage, interviews, conference appearances, published posts. This is where strategy and current concerns surface.

**4b. Public financial filings, where they exist.** Registry accounts, consolidated reports, listed-company disclosures. For any business large enough to publish audited or consolidated statements, the filing outranks every aggregator — it carries headcount, entity structure, ownership and segment detail that no directory has. Primary source; label it as one.

**5. Social and professional networks.** Company pages carry size bands and locations. Note that some networks block automated fetching; if a source will not serve the content, record it as unavailable rather than working around the restriction.

Stop when a new search returns nothing new. Two consecutive empty searches is the convention this skill uses — a working stopping rule, not a measured optimum. Adopt a different one if it suits you, but adopt one, because the alternative is researching until the effort feels sufficient, which has no end.

## What each source can and cannot establish

| Source | Establishes | Does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Company website | What they claim about themselves, service range, self-described positioning, named leadership | Whether any claim is true, actual volumes, internal tools, team size |
| Registry | Legal existence, entity name, officers of record, address, status **as of a date** | Current reality — registries lag, sometimes by years |
| Trade aggregators | That certain shipments were observed | Total activity. Coverage is partial and silence means nothing. |
| News | What was said publicly and when | Whether it still holds |
| Job adverts | Tools, team structure, reporting lines, growth areas | Whether the role was filled |
| Directories | Existence, sector, sometimes size band | Anything precise |

The recurring error is treating an aggregator's silence as evidence. "No exports recorded" means that provider recorded none, and writing it as though the company does not export is a claim you cannot support and the client will find insulting.

## Grading a claim

Every line in the dossier gets one of three grades. The grade drives the citation format.

**CONFIRMED** — stated by a primary source, or by two independent sources that agree. Cite the source.

**CLAIMED** — the company says it about itself and no independent source confirms it. Cite as *the company states*. Most impressive numbers on a company website are this grade, and there is nothing wrong with that as long as the reader can see it.

**UNVERIFIED** — you believe it, you cannot show it. Mark it `UNVERIFIED` and keep it only if it is useful as a question. Otherwise leave it out.

Inference never gets a grade. "They trade in West Africa, so they probably deal in letters of credit" is a question for the call, not a line in the dossier. The test is simple: if you cannot point at where you read it, it is not a finding.

## Fidelity — the question the three grades do not ask

All three grades answer the same question: *whose assertion is this?* None asks *does the source say it as strongly as I wrote it?* — and on the one run where both were measured, the second question is where six of ten factual errors turned out to live. A verbatim transcription and a quietly upgraded paraphrase of the same registry line grade identically, carry the same label, and pass every automated check.

So: where the source has its own noun, its own verb or its own field name, that word is the one that goes in the dossier. A stronger word is a different claim wearing the same citation, and it is harder to see than an invented one, because each substitution looks harmless on its own.

Four from that run, all caught only on a second reading:

- A registry field labelled **"Delatnost"** became **"pretežna delatnost"**. The page has no such word and no activity code; a second aggregator gave a different code entirely. That one upgrade turned a company into a registered producer rather than a trader, and three downstream inferences rested on it.
- Registry fields **"Zastupnici"** and **"Vlasnici"** became **"osnivač"**. Owner is not founder, and the page never says founder.
- A job advert's **"dostavlja uzorke"** became **"priprema uzoraka"**. Delivering samples is not preparing them.
- **"Preko 100m"**, with no currency stated anywhere on the page, became **"preko 100 miliona dinara"**. Note what that did: supplying the currency made the figure *more* legible to the automated checker and *less* true.

Two corollaries with the same root:

**A list restated is restated whole**, in the source's own items, or marked as partial. That run dropped two sales channels the company named and added one it never mentioned — and the invented channel was the one that would have been said aloud on the call.

**A derived figure is something you computed, not something you read.** A count, a range, a minimum, a maximum. It inherits the citation of the page it came from and then stops looking derived, which is how a struck-through pre-discount price became a catalogue maximum with a clean citation behind it. Say what you counted over and what you excluded; that sentence is the one that catches it.

**Platform furniture is not the source's text.** A badge the job board puts beside every advert, a breadcrumb, a template footer — none of it is the advertiser speaking. The same run recorded "remote work possible" as a stated condition of a role when it was a site-wide badge. If you cannot tell whose words they are, that is a gap, not a finding.

## Handling contradictions

Contradictions are among the most valuable things research produces, and the instinct to tidy them away is exactly wrong.

When the site says the business was founded in 1986 and the registry says the company was incorporated in 2018, that is not an error to resolve — it is a group structure, a restructuring or a rebrand, and which one it is changes who employs whom and which entity holds the data. Record both, cite both, and put the question on the agenda.

When the contradiction points at a **group**, research it rather than deferring all of it. Which entity employs the people, which holds the data and which would sign are answerable from filings and registry records, and they decide who your counterparty is. Record the group as far as it is visible, and take to the call only what the documents cannot settle.

Same for a name that differs between site and registry, officers listed in one place and not the other, and addresses that disagree. Present the contradiction, name the two sources, and let the call settle it. A dossier that quietly picks a winner hides the most interesting question in it.

## Time-boxing

A dossier is a preparation artefact, not a research project. Somewhere around a dozen fetches and searches the return flattens sharply, because the remaining unknowns are operational — how the team works, what takes the longest, who decides — and no amount of searching answers those.

When you find yourself looking for a third confirmation of something already confirmed, or reading a fifth page that repeats the first four, stop. That effort belongs in the call agenda instead.

## When research contradicts the person who asked

The same discipline as source-versus-source, with one addition: say so before you go further.

If the brief says a fifty-person logistics firm and the registry shows a three-person dormant company, if two companies share the name, or if the spelling resolves to nothing, stop and report it. Do not quietly research the entity that seems more likely — a run spent on the wrong company produces four confident, useless documents, and the person who asked is the only one who can say which company they meant.

Where the difference is smaller — a headcount, a market, a role — record both, cite the source, and raise it as a question rather than correcting them in a document.
