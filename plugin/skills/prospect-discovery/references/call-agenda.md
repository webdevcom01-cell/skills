# The discovery call agenda

## The one test for a question

**Could this question be asked of any company in this sector?** If yes, cut it.

"What are your biggest challenges?" wastes a slot and signals you did no preparation. "You publish FX sourcing and customs documentation as separate services — is that the same person doing both, or two teams?" cannot be asked of anyone else, proves you read their material, and gets a specific answer that changes what you propose.

The dossier's section 8 is the raw material. Each operational unknown becomes a question, phrased so it is obvious you already know the surrounding context.

**Make the test an act, not a principle.** Stated as a principle it loses, every time, to six named blocks with minute budgets waiting to be filled — on the run this guidance comes from, seven of twenty questions failed the test in a pack whose author had read it. So: every finished question names the specific published thing it stands on. The customs warehouse. The WooCommerce shop. The three site languages. The one software named in their job advert. A question that cannot name one is the generic question wearing a local coat, and the prospect can hear the difference.

## Structure

Sized for a 60-minute call and totalling 50 minutes, which leaves room to follow an answer sideways. In a 45-minute slot, drop the tools block to five minutes and take the rest from the close; the best material comes from following an answer sideways, and an agenda packed to the minute prevents that.

Each block below lists **the ground to cover, not the questions to ask**. That distinction is where agendas go wrong: a block naming six topics reads as a licence to write six questions, and a ten-minute block that survives contact with a real answer carries two or three. Write fewer than the ground suggests.

### Opening — 5 minutes

State what you understood about their business from public material, in three sentences, and ask what you got wrong.

This does more work than anything else on the agenda. It proves preparation, it corrects your errors before they reach a proposal, and people enjoy correcting an outsider who is nearly right. It also surfaces the group-structure and history questions naturally rather than as interrogation.

### Their words for the work — 10 minutes

What the team actually does day to day, in their vocabulary. You are listening for the repeated tasks, and you need them in their own phrasing, because that phrasing goes into every downstream document and a client notices immediately when it is not theirs.

Useful shapes: *walk me through what happens from an enquiry arriving to an offer going out. Who touches it? What do they wait for?*

### Tools and systems — 10 minutes

What they work in, which system is the system of record when two overlap, what was bought and never adopted, what has been tried before and failed.

That last one is the highest-value question on the agenda and the one people forget. A prospect who has already had a failed attempt will not say so unless asked, and everything you propose lands differently depending on the answer.

### Where the time and the pain go — 10 minutes

Which task takes longest, which is most disliked — they are usually different, and the difference predicts what will actually get adopted. What breaks. What has to be redone.

Do not ask for numbers here; you will not get reliable ones in a call, and asking implies you will take them at face value. Measurement is a later step with a method behind it.

This rubs against `references/dossier.md` §8, which tells you to record *how long anything takes* as an operational unknown — and section 8 is what feeds this agenda, so the pipeline will hand you "how long does X take?" unless you catch it. Both rules are right. The duration is a legitimate gap and the reason this block exists; the question that gets a usable answer is **"walk me through it"**, not "how long does it take". You are converting the gap into an observation to make, not a figure to request — and you will learn more from where someone pauses in the walkthrough than from any number they would have guessed.

### Decision and constraints — 10 minutes

Who decides, who else must agree, what budget cycle they are in, what has to be true for this to happen, what would stop it. Regulatory or contractual constraints on their data.

Ask directly. A second approver nobody named is a common way for an agreed engagement to stall — common enough to be worth a direct question, on the working assumption rather than on evidence we can cite.

### Close — 5 minutes

Confirm what you heard, name the single thing you would look at first and why, and agree the next step with a date on it.

## What to bring, and what to hold back

Bring the two or three most specific things you learned. Used well — *you moved into road trade across Central Africa around 2019, was that a logistics response or a market one?* — this is the strongest opening available, because it is a real question you could only ask having read their material.

Hold back the exhaustive list. Reciting everything you found reads as surveillance rather than preparation, and it puts the prospect on the defensive in the first five minutes. Two or three well-chosen details prove the work; ten prove something else.

Never open with a contradiction you found. Save the founding-date or entity questions for the middle of the call, framed as curiosity about structure rather than as a discrepancy you caught them in.

## After the call

Update the dossier the same day, moving items out of section 8 as they are answered and correcting anything the prospect corrected. The intake block for the engagement skill fills itself from that update — which is the entire point of having kept the gaps explicit.
