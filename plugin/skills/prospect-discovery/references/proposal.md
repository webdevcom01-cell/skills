# The proposal skeleton

## Why a skeleton and not a proposal

A proposal written before the discovery call is a guess with a price on it. Its purpose here is to be **the thing you fill in during and after the call**, so that a prospect who says "send me something" gets it the same day rather than a week later.

Everything commercial is a hypothesis until the call tests it. Label it that way, in the document, where you will see it while writing.

## Structure

### 1. What we understood

Three or four sentences on their business, from the dossier, cited. This paragraph is doing the same work as the call opening: proving you looked, and inviting correction.

**Cited means inline labels, the same as in the dossier — and the grade travels with the line.** A figure the dossier carries as "the company states [own site]" arrives here as the company's own claim, or it does not arrive at all. Dropped in transit it becomes *your* assertion about them, in the one document they actually read, and you get to defend a number you never verified. A blanket footnote saying the claims are sourced in an internal file is not a citation; it is the shortcut the run behind this note took, and it emptied the only client-facing file of every label it should have had. Run `check_sources.py --strict` on this file too.

### 2. Where we think the repetitive work sits — HYPOTHESES

Each one labelled, each traceable to something published, each phrased so it can be wrong:

```markdown
**Hypothesis 1 — TO TEST ON CALL.** Their published service list separates FX sourcing
and documentation from customs clearing [source]. Cross-border administration of this
kind often concentrates in a few people doing it repeatedly — our working assumption
from similar businesses, not a measured finding. Wrong if this is outsourced to an agent.
```

The "wrong if" clause is not decoration. A hypothesis you have not said how to falsify is a sales pitch, and clients recognise the difference.

### 3. What we would look at first — and why

One thing, not five. Name it, say why it is first, say what would change the answer.

Prospects trust a consultant who has a first move more than one who presents options. Options say you have not decided; a first move with a reason says you have, and it invites the conversation that corrects it if you are wrong.

### 4. How an engagement would be shaped

The shape only: what happens, in what order, over roughly what period, who from their side is needed. Point at the engagement skill's programme structure if that is the intended route.

Not a week-by-week plan. Writing one before you know their tasks produces the generic document research is meant to prevent.

### 5. What we would need from you

The unknowns from the dossier's section 8, in client-facing language. Doing this in the proposal rather than after signature sets the expectation that the engagement has dependencies, which is worth more later than it costs now.

### 6. What this would not cover

Legal and compliance advice, IT security, tool licences, building or operating anything. State it now.

Saying it before you are asked reads as confidence rather than hedging, and it protects the eventual engagement from an expectation nobody wrote down.

### 7. Next step

One, with a date.

## No fee appears in this document

Scope follows the call; fees follow scope. A number written before either is a number you will have to defend or retract, and both are worse than a blank.

If the prospect asks for a range before the call — and a decisive one will — give it verbally, framed as a range with the conditions attached. Do not write it into the skeleton, where it hardens into a commitment neither side agreed to.

## Rules on claims

The same restrictions that govern the engagement deliverables apply here, and a proposal is where they are most tempting to break:

- **No claim that anything is compliant, lawful, defensible or approved.** Not about them, not about what you would build.
- **No named statute or regulator you cannot cite.**
- **No industry statistic without the study, its sample, and the criticism of it.** "Most AI projects fail" is exactly the unsourced claim you are selling the discipline against; using it in the pitch is self-defeating and a sharp prospect will say so.
- **No promise of an outcome you do not control.** Commit to work delivered, not to a result.
- **No invented fact about them.** If the dossier does not have it with a source, the proposal does not assert it.
