## Adverse findings, and when to stop

Research sometimes turns up litigation, insolvency, enforcement action, sanctions or press alleging wrongdoing. The instinct to write it into the dossier under "Signals" is wrong, and this is the one place where the skill's own bias toward recording everything does not apply.

- **Do not paraphrase an allegation.** If it goes in at all, it goes in as the source's own words, quoted, dated, attributed, with the outcome if there is one — and never as your characterisation of what happened. A paraphrase of an allegation about a named individual, in a file you email, is the worst artefact this skill can produce.
- **Separate the fact from the allegation.** "A filing exists" and "they did the thing alleged" are different claims with different evidence.
- **Escalate rather than write.** Anything suggesting sanctions exposure, an enforcement action or an insolvency stops the research and becomes a conversation with the user about whether to proceed at all. That is a commercial and legal decision, not a research one.
- **Check for a conflict before you start.** If the prospect competes with a current client, the engagement may be closed to you regardless of what the research says. Ask that question before the first fetch, not after the dossier exists.

## What not to research

Three boundaries, all worth holding.

**Individuals beyond their public professional role.** A named officer's role, their published statements and their professional history at the company are in scope. Their personal social media, home address, family, health, politics and anything else about their private life are not, however findable.

The grey case is a professional profile that mixes role with education, location and career history. The workable test: **would you be comfortable reading this line aloud to the person on the call?** Role and employer, yes. Where they live, no.

**Registered addresses need care.** For sole traders and small companies the registered address is frequently someone's home. Record it as the registered address of the company, do not comment on it, and do not resolve it to a person.

**Anything behind a login, a paywall or a robots restriction.** If a source will not serve the content, note it as unavailable and move on. Note also that some platforms prohibit automated access in their terms while serving pages freely — serving is not permission. Working around an access restriction to research a company you intend to sell to is a poor foundation for the relationship, and may breach those terms. Whether it carries any further consequence is a question for your own adviser, not something this skill can tell you.

## Handling the personal data you collect

A dossier on a named company is also a small file on named living people, and it is built from third-party sources rather than from them. That engages data-protection duties in most of Europe — including notice, a lawful basis, and retention limits — and the specifics depend on your jurisdiction and theirs.

**This skill does not tell you what the law requires of you, and no deliverable it produces may claim compliance.** What it does is refuse to make the position worse:

- Collect the minimum that serves the commercial purpose. Officer names and roles, yes; anything further, only with a reason you could state out loud.
- Keep the dossier in one known place, not scattered across chats and drafts.
- **Set a deletion date when you create it** — 90 days after the call is a defensible default — and put that date in the file, so a dossier nobody acted on does not sit around for years.
- Delete it when the opportunity closes.
- If the prospect asks what you hold about them, be able to answer.

Have your own adviser confirm what applies to you, once, and record the answer. It is the same discipline this skill asks of everything else: a rule you can point at beats a habit you cannot.
